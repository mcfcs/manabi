Below is a study-guide style analysis of **Michael Barnett and Raymond Duvall, “Power in Global Governance,” Chapter 1**. The chapter’s central argument is that discussions of global governance often treat cooperation, institutions, and shared values as if they operate separately from power. Barnett and Duvall argue that this is misleading: **power is embedded in virtually every aspect of global governance**, and power itself takes several distinct forms. 

# 1. Opening: Why Power Matters in Global Governance

### Brief summary

The chapter begins by explaining how **global governance** became a major concept after the Cold War. Globalization increased economic, political, environmental, and security connections among states and peoples, creating a perceived need for international rules, institutions, and mechanisms of cooperation.

Global governance is often presented positively: as a way to solve collective problems, reduce conflict, protect human rights, and promote shared interests.

However, the authors identify a major problem:

> **Studies of global governance often fail to seriously analyze power.**

This is strange because governance itself involves rules, institutions, structures, and mechanisms that regulate social behavior—and therefore inevitably involves power. 

### Key points

* Global governance expanded greatly as a concept after the Cold War.
* It is strongly associated with globalization and international cooperation.
* Scholars frequently emphasize:

  * cooperation
  * coordination
  * institutions
  * shared interests
  * common values
* But they often understate:

  * domination
  * inequality
  * exclusion
  * coercion
  * structural advantages

### What this section is conveying

The authors are warning us not to think:

**Global governance = neutral international cooperation.**

Instead:

**Global governance = cooperation + institutions + rules + values + power relations.**

---

# 2. The Problem with the Traditional View of Power

International relations has traditionally been heavily influenced by **realism**.

In the realist conception, power usually means something like:

> **State A gets State B to do something that State B otherwise would not do.**

This typically focuses on material resources such as:

* military power
* economic strength
* sanctions
* threats
* coercion

Barnett and Duvall argue that this definition is too narrow.

Power does not only occur when one state openly threatens another. Power can also operate through:

* international institutions
* economic structures
* social positions
* accepted knowledge
* language
* norms
* classifications
* ideas about what is “normal” or “legitimate”

Therefore, focusing only on direct coercion actually causes scholars to **underestimate how much power exists in international politics**. 

---

# 3. The Authors' Definition of Power

This is probably the **most important definition to memorize**:

> **Power is the production, in and through social relations, of effects that shape the capacities of actors to determine their own circumstances and fate.** 

In simpler words:

**Power exists whenever social relationships affect what actors can do, become, choose, or achieve.**

So power is not just:

**“Can A force B?”**

It is also:

**“What social relationships make certain actions possible or impossible for A and B?”**

---

# 4. The Two Dimensions of Power

The authors create their four types of power by crossing **two dimensions**.

## Dimension 1: Interaction vs. Constitution

### Interaction

Power operates through the behavior of already-existing actors.

Example:

> A state threatens another state.

A and B already exist; the question is how A affects B.

This is closely connected to:

### **Power over**

One actor exercises control over another.

---

### Constitution

Power helps create or shape the actors themselves.

Instead of asking:

> “What does A make B do?”

we ask:

> “How did social structures make A and B into these kinds of actors in the first place?”

This is closer to:

### **Power to**

Social relations determine actors' capacities, identities, and possibilities.

For example, capitalism helps constitute actors as:

* capitalists
* workers

Those positions come with different opportunities and capacities.

---

# 5. Dimension 2: Direct vs. Diffuse

The second distinction concerns **how immediate the relationship is**.

### Direct / Specific

There is an identifiable connection between actors.

Example:

> A government directly threatens another government.

---

### Diffuse / Indirect

Power works through broader institutions, rules, traditions, or systems.

Example:

An international institution may contain rules developed decades ago that continue to privilege some states over others.

There may be no single actor actively saying:

> “I am controlling you.”

Yet power still operates.

---

# 6. The Four Types of Power

Combining these two dimensions gives the chapter's most important framework.

|                  | **Direct**           | **Diffuse**             |
| ---------------- | -------------------- | ----------------------- |
| **Interaction**  | **Compulsory Power** | **Institutional Power** |
| **Constitution** | **Structural Power** | **Productive Power**    |



A useful way to memorize them is:

**CISP**

**C**ompulsory
**I**nstitutional
**S**tructural
**P**roductive

---

# 7. Compulsory Power

## Definition

**Direct control of one actor over another.**

The classic formulation comes from Robert Dahl:

> A has power over B when A gets B to do something B otherwise would not do.

Barnett and Duvall broaden this slightly: the effect does **not necessarily have to be intentional**.

If A's actions directly affect B's circumstances, compulsory power may still exist even if A did not intend all of those consequences. 

### Examples

* military threats
* economic sanctions
* powerful states pressuring weaker states
* corporations pressuring governments
* World Bank pressure on borrowing countries
* international organizations imposing conditions

But compulsory power is **not only material**.

It can also involve:

* shaming
* reputation
* moral pressure
* symbolic pressure
* legal norms

For example, NGOs may shame governments into changing policies.

### Simplest formulation

**A directly makes B do something.**

### Key word

**Direct control**

---

# 8. Institutional Power

## Definition

**Indirect control exercised through institutions, rules, procedures, and arrangements.**

Instead of:

**A → B**

it looks more like:

**A → Institution → B**

The institution mediates the relationship.



Institutions can include:

* formal organizations
* international agreements
* decision-making rules
* procedures
* systems of exchange
* agenda-setting mechanisms

### Important idea: Institutional bias

Institutions are not automatically neutral.

Their rules may:

* benefit certain countries
* disadvantage others
* determine who can participate
* exclude certain issues
* determine whose interests count
* create long-term winners and losers

Sometimes these effects persist even after the original creators are gone.

The authors describe old institutions as potentially becoming:

> **“frozen configurations of privilege and bias.”**

### Agenda-setting

Institutional power also involves determining:

> **What can even be discussed?**

If an institution prevents an issue from reaching the agenda, power is already being exercised.

### Simplest formulation

**Rules and institutions indirectly shape what actors can do.**

### Key word

**Indirect control**

---

# 9. Structural Power

## Definition

Structural power concerns social relationships that **constitute actors and their capacities in direct relation to each other**.

Classic examples include:

* master ↔ slave
* capitalist ↔ worker
* capital ↔ labor

One position exists in relation to the other.



The important difference from institutional power is this:

### Institutional power

Actors already exist with preferences.

The rules then affect what they can do.

### Structural power

The structure helps create:

* who the actors are
* their interests
* their capacities
* their social positions

---

### Unequal capacities

Structural positions often give actors unequal abilities.

For instance:

A capitalist and a worker are both part of capitalist production, but they possess very different resources and capacities.

---

### Power and self-understanding

Structural power can go even deeper.

It may affect how people understand their own interests.

Someone may accept an unequal position because the social structure makes that arrangement appear:

* normal
* natural
* legitimate
* unavoidable

This connects to **Steven Lukes' idea** that one of the deepest forms of power is shaping people's perceptions so that they do not even recognize their domination.

### Simplest formulation

**Social structures create unequal positions and capacities.**

### Key word

**Position**

---

# 10. Productive Power

Productive power is perhaps the most abstract of the four.

## Definition

**Power that works through diffuse systems of knowledge, meaning, discourse, and classification to produce social identities and possibilities.**



It is strongly influenced by **Michel Foucault**.

Instead of simply asking:

> Who controls whom?

productive power asks:

> How does society create categories through which we understand people, states, problems, and acceptable behavior?

---

## Discourse

An especially important term is:

### **Discourse**

A discourse is not merely conversation.

It is a broader **system of meaning and knowledge** that determines what appears:

* normal
* legitimate
* possible
* impossible
* true
* false
* problematic

---

### Examples of productive power

International politics uses categories such as:

* “civilized”
* “rogue state”
* “Western”
* “unstable”
* “democratic”
* “developed”
* “developing”
* “civilian”
* “combatant”

These categories do more than describe reality.

They help **produce political reality** because they influence:

* who receives legitimacy
* who is viewed as threatening
* whose knowledge matters
* who deserves protection
* who may legitimately act

---

### Development

The authors give development as an example.

If one particular definition of “development” becomes dominant, then it determines:

* what counts as progress
* what problems governments should solve
* which experts are credible
* which policies are acceptable

Thus knowledge itself becomes connected to power.

### Simplest formulation

**Ideas and discourse shape identities and determine what seems possible or legitimate.**

### Key word

**Meaning**

---

# 11. Structural vs. Productive Power

This distinction is easy to confuse.

### Structural Power

Focuses on **specific social positions in relation to one another**.

Example:

> capitalist ↔ worker

---

### Productive Power

Focuses on **broader systems of meaning and discourse**.

Example:

> What does society mean by “developed country”?

or:

> What makes someone count as a “civilian”?

A useful shortcut:

**Structural = positions**

**Productive = meanings**

---

# 12. Power and Liberal Global Governance

One of the chapter's major criticisms concerns **liberalism**.

After the Cold War, global governance became closely associated with liberal values such as:

* democracy
* human rights
* rule of law
* free markets
* international institutions
* cooperation
* individual freedom

These may be desirable values, but Barnett and Duvall argue that presenting them simply as shared universal values can hide the **power relationships behind them**.



For example:

International institutions might promote free trade.

But we should still ask:

* Who designed the rules?
* Who benefits most?
* Who has to adjust?
* Who gets to interpret those rules?
* Whose idea of “fair trade” becomes dominant?

Therefore, liberal global governance may simultaneously promote cooperation **and reproduce inequalities**.

---

# 13. Why the Traditional Study of Global Governance Misses Power

The authors identify several reasons.

### 1. Post-Cold War optimism

Global governance emerged during a period of optimism about international cooperation.

### 2. Liberalism

Scholars emphasized:

* cooperation
* institutions
* common interests
* shared values

rather than domination.

### 3. Opposition to realism

Many liberal and constructivist scholars wanted to prove that international politics could not be explained simply by military and material power.

Ironically, by distancing themselves from realist conceptions of power, some scholars began neglecting **power altogether**.

The authors' solution is not to return entirely to realism.

Instead:

> **Expand the meaning of power.**

---

# 14. Power and Resistance

Another important section explains that **power generates resistance**.

Since power affects actors' ability to control their own circumstances, those disadvantaged by power often try to challenge it.

Each type of power therefore corresponds to different kinds of resistance. 

### Compulsory power → direct counteraction

If one actor directly controls another, the weaker actor may develop capabilities to resist.

Example:

States balancing against a dominant power.

---

### Institutional power → change the rules

Resistance may attempt to alter:

* institutional rules
* procedures
* representation
* agendas
* distribution of benefits

Example:

NGOs trying to change international human-rights law.

---

### Structural power → transform unequal relationships

Actors in subordinate positions may organize collectively to change structural inequalities.

Examples:

* labor movements
* anti-globalization movements
* decolonization movements

---

### Productive power → challenge meanings and identities

Resistance may challenge dominant categories or discourses.

Example:

Colonized peoples could use liberal ideas like:

* equality
* freedom
* self-determination

against colonialism itself.

This demonstrates an important point:

**Discourses that generate power can also provide resources for resistance.**

---

# 15. The Relationship Among the Four Types

The authors do **not** claim that only one form of power operates in any situation.

This is extremely important.

Instead, the four types often operate **together**.

For example, an international organization may have:

* **compulsory power** — directly pressures states
* **institutional power** — creates rules that shape behavior
* **productive power** — defines legitimate knowledge and categories

Similarly, capitalism may contain:

* structural power
* institutional power
* productive power
* sometimes compulsory power

Therefore, the taxonomy is not intended to create four isolated boxes.

Its purpose is to help us see **how different forms of power interact**.

---

# 16. Organization of the Rest of the Book

The final portion of Chapter 1 previews later chapters.

You probably do not need to memorize every author, but understand what the book will investigate.

The later chapters examine power through areas such as:

* international organizations
* international trade
* WTO
* IMF
* global capitalism
* policing
* international humanitarian law
* global civil society
* UN Security Council
* World Economic Forum

The common theme is that **liberal global governance contains multiple forms of power**.

# The Four Powers — Fast Memorization Version

For exams, this is probably the easiest version to remember:

### Compulsory

**“Do this or else.”**

A directly controls B.

---

### Institutional

**“These are the rules.”**

Institutions indirectly shape B's options.

---

### Structural

**“This is your position in the system.”**

Social relationships create unequal roles and capacities.

---

### Productive

**“This is how reality is understood.”**

Discourse and knowledge define identities, problems, and possibilities.

# What Is the Chapter Ultimately Conveying?

The chapter is making a broader argument about how we should understand politics.

Global governance is often described as though international institutions simply help countries:

> cooperate → solve problems → create common rules → achieve common interests.

Barnett and Duvall argue that this picture is incomplete.

A better picture is:

> **Global governance organizes international life, and whenever social life is organized, power is involved.**

Power determines:

* who can participate
* who makes decisions
* whose interests are protected
* whose knowledge is accepted
* which choices are possible
* what identities exist
* what counts as legitimate behavior
* who benefits
* who bears costs

Therefore, studying global governance requires asking not only:

> **“Does this institution produce cooperation?”**

but also:

> **“Who is empowered by this arrangement, who is constrained, and through what form of power?”**

---

# Whole-Chapter Summary

Barnett and Duvall argue that **power must be placed at the center of the study of global governance**. Although global governance became increasingly important after the Cold War because globalization created greater demand for international cooperation and institutions, scholars often approached it through a liberal framework emphasizing common interests, coordination, progress, and shared values. This orientation unintentionally obscured many of the inequalities and power relations embedded within global institutions.

The authors also criticize the dominant realist definition of power as too narrow. Realism tends to understand power mainly as one state using material capabilities to compel another state to change its behavior. Barnett and Duvall instead define power broadly as the production of effects through social relations that shape actors' ability to determine their circumstances and fate.

Using two dimensions—**interaction versus constitution** and **direct versus diffuse relations**—they develop four types of power. **Compulsory power** is direct control by one actor over another. **Institutional power** operates indirectly through institutions and rules. **Structural power** operates through social relations that constitute actors in unequal positions and give them different interests and capacities. **Productive power** works through discourse, knowledge, categories, and systems of meaning that shape identities and determine what appears legitimate or possible.

These forms of power frequently operate together rather than separately. International organizations, markets, laws, states, and global civil society can therefore simultaneously exercise several kinds of power. Furthermore, every form of power can generate corresponding forms of resistance, ranging from direct opposition to institutional reform, structural transformation, and challenges to dominant discourses.

Ultimately, the chapter argues that global governance should not simply be evaluated according to whether it creates efficient cooperation. Scholars must also examine **who benefits, who loses, whose voices matter, what ideas become dominant, and whether the institutions governing global life are legitimate and fair**. 

# Good “What Are Your Thoughts on the Reading?” Answer

A strong response could be:

I think the most important contribution of the reading is that it expands our understanding of power beyond the obvious use of military or economic force. Before looking at Barnett and Duvall's framework, it is easy to think of international power mainly as one powerful country forcing a weaker country to do something. The chapter shows that power can be much more subtle: institutions can shape the choices available to states, economic structures can place actors in unequal positions, and even accepted ideas and categories can determine whose voices or knowledge are considered legitimate.

I also find the argument important because it challenges the assumption that global governance is automatically neutral or beneficial simply because it promotes cooperation. An international institution may genuinely solve collective problems while still creating winners and losers or privileging certain countries, interests, and ideas. This means that cooperation and power are not opposites; power can actually be embedded within cooperation itself.

Ultimately, the reading made me think that whenever we examine an institution, law, or international system, we should ask not only **“Does it work?”** but also **“Who benefits from the way it works, who is constrained by it, and who had the power to define the rules in the first place?”** That makes Barnett and Duvall's framework useful not only for international relations but for understanding politics more generally.
